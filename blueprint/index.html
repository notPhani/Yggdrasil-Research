§1 · the whole machine

Four units, four contracts, one clock

For a ten-year-old

Picture a detective who reads every newspaper in the world, every fifteen minutes, long before anything goes wrong. The detective keeps a giant map of stories: which ones people are talking about, and which stories make other stories louder.

One day a toy company's price suddenly crashes. The detective may use only the newspapers they had already read before the crash, because peeking at tomorrow's paper is cheating. Using the map, the detective finds the shortest chains of stories that could explain the crash. Then the detective goes looking for clues that would prove each chain wrong. At the end they say which chains survived and which didn't, or they honestly say "I don't know".

Yggdrasil is one deterministic program that steps through time in 15-minute windows. Each step reads one GDELT batch, updates the world model, and checks the market. When the market does something abnormal, it opens a case and searches the frozen world model as it stood at the moment the move began. The world model is built without regard to any stock. Explanations are reads from that state, not fresh investigations. design

The one-way valveTargeted pages are evidence, never attention. Without the valve, the search would choose what to fetch, the fetched pages would count as attention, and the model would get stronger on the very hypothesis it was asked to test.

One clock decides what counts as evidence

Every record carries four clocks that are never collapsed into one. event_time is when the thing happened. published_time is the outlet's timestamp, present for about 59% of GKG documents via PAGE_PRECISEPUBTIMESTAMP measured. observed_time is the batch time. ingested_time is observed_time + 15 min in a replay, which removes the wall clock. Admissibility uses a fifth, derived clock:

\[ \text{first_seen}(o) = \min_{o' \equiv o} \text{ingested_time}(o'), \qquad \text{admissible}(o, \tau^*) \iff \text{first_seen}(o) \lt \tau^* \]

Here \(o' \equiv o\) runs over every record with the same exact key (native id, canonical URL or content hash). A SerpApi page fetched in October 2026 has first_seen = October 2026, which would make it useless in a replay. Decision 0.6 fixes this: if its canonical URL matches a GDELT record, it inherits that record's first_seen. Otherwise it goes only into \(P_{\text{all}}\), the program that judges truth, and never into \(P_{\text{pre}}\), the program that judges what moved the price.

Proof · Theorem 3.1, end-to-end replay determinism

Let \(x_0, x_1, \dots\) be the archived inputs (raw blobs plus archived model and API outputs) sorted by the D4 key, and let every stage be a state map \(s_{n+1} = \Phi(s_n, x_n, \text{cfg})\). D1 removes the clock, D2 the RNG state, D3 the reduction order, D4 container iteration order, and D5 the outside world. By induction every state is a function of the inputs and the config, and every output is a projection of a state. QED

Rounding scores before threshold tests does not provide this guarantee, because a value can straddle a grid line. The guarantee comes from D3. §2 · Engine 1 · Observation · Session 1, frozen

Turn the firehose into deduplicated, time-stamped objects

For a ten-year-old

Every fifteen minutes a truck dumps a mountain of newspapers at the library. Lots of them are photocopies of the same article that different towns printed. Engine 1 is the librarian. It stamps every paper with the time it arrived and throws out exact repeats. It staples the photocopies together and writes on the front "15 towns printed this".

It never staples together two different reporters who wrote about the same thing. Two reporters writing means more people care, and caring is exactly what the next engine wants to measure.

The four-step semantic cascade, cheapest first design · frozen 2026-10-05

Candidates. A pair (o, c) is considered only if c is earlier in the D4 order, within 72 hours, and either their normalized titles are equal or their title embeddings are close:

\[ \operatorname{cand}(o, c) \iff t_c \le t_o \le t_c + 72\,\text{h} \;\wedge\; \big[\, \operatorname{norm}(\text{title}_o) = \operatorname{norm}(\text{title}_c) \;\vee\; \cos(e_o, e_c) \ge \theta_{\text{cand}} \,\big] \]

Proof · Lemma 4.1, the SimHash index has exact recall at distance 3 derived

Split 64-bit fingerprints into four 16-bit blocks. If \(\operatorname{ham}(f_1, f_2) \le 3\), at most three blocks contain a differing bit, so at least one block is identical. Probing the four block tables therefore returns every pair within distance 3. Acceptance is decided by computing the exact Hamming distance, so the set of accepted pairs is a deterministic function of the fingerprints. This is the block-permutation idea of Manku, Jain and Das Sarma (2007), who used k = 3 on 64-bit fingerprints for 8 billion pages. QED

Invariant · L3 is never merged design

Every merge rule requires evidence of copying: equal canonical declarations, near-identical body text, or an identical title plus an identical image, byline or name set. Two reporters writing their own stories share none of these at the thresholds above. So independent coverage, which is the attention signal, survives Engine 1 by construction. The one exception is step 4 on two independently written articles with the same title, the same byline and overlapping names. That is rare, and it is logged as method = A so it can be audited.

Storage (decision 2.7b)

  • Raw zips are deleted after extraction. The manifest line (url, md5, size) is kept, and since GDELT keeps every file online since 2015, a replay can re-fetch with an md5 check.
  • Embeddings are the real space hog: 1,536 B as float32, more than seven times a whole document record. They are stored as int8 (384 B) for the 72-hour dedup window only, then deleted. The cached dedup decisions remain (D5).
  • Page heads and bodies are kept for 7 days, then replaced by their MinHash and SimHash signatures (520 B).
  • Kept forever: the document table, the manifests, and every item a dossier cites.
  • Query: DuckDB views over day-partitioned Parquet, e.g. SELECT min(first_seen) FROM obs_doc WHERE url_key = ? for admissibility.

§3 · Stock observer · Session 2, locked (2.3′ amended)

Decide that something abnormal happened, and pin the moment it started

For a ten-year-old

This is a smoke alarm for stocks. It goes off only when two things are both true. First, a stock moved far more than the whole market did that day. Second, something confirms it: lots of extra trading, or a big jump the moment trading opened. One clue alone isn't enough.

The alarm never reads the news, so it can't be fooled by headlines. When it rings, it writes down the very first moment the smoke appeared. The detective may only use clues from before that moment.

Clusters: one case per day, one terminal per cluster design · 2.6

\[ i \sim j \iff \operatorname{corr}(\hat\varepsilon_i, \hat\varepsilon_j) \ge \rho_0 \;\wedge\; \operatorname{sign}(AR_i) = \operatorname{sign}(AR_j), \qquad \text{clusters} = \text{connected components of } \sim \text{ on fired instruments} \]

ETFs are members but never terminals. Opening shocks are secondary members: formalized for this doc \(\text{secondary}(x) \iff |M_g| \ge 5 \wedge \neg\text{fire}(x)\). That catches MSFT (gap −4.5%, \(M_g = -9.1\)), ASML, MU and GOOGL, which are listed as evidence but are not search terminals. One terminal per cluster turns 9 terminals into \(k = 2\), so the search's \(3^k\) factor drops from 19,683 to 9. It also makes the search answer the right question: does one shared root explain both clusters more cheaply than two separate stories?

Cross-sectional statistics get the Kolari–Pynnönen correction for same-day clustering:

\[ z_{\text{adj}} = z \sqrt{\frac{1 - \bar r}{1 + (N - 1)\,\bar r}}, \qquad \bar r = \text{mean pairwise residual correlation} \]
Why the correction · equicorrelated standardized returns proven · Kolari & Pynnönen 2010

If \(N\) standardized abnormal returns each have variance \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise correlation \(\bar r\), their mean has variance \(\sigma^2 (1 + (N-1)\bar r)/N\), not \(\sigma^2/N\). The usual cross-sectional variance estimator has expectation \(\sigma^2 (1 - \bar r)\). The ratio of the true variance to the naive one is \((1 + (N-1)\bar r)/(1 - \bar r)\), and \(z_{\text{adj}}\) divides by its square root. With \(N = 9\) and \(\bar r = 0.3\) the factor is \(\sqrt{0.7/3.4} \approx 0.45\): an unadjusted test would overstate significance more than twofold.

The cutoff τ* design · 2.5

\[ \tau^*(\text{case}) = \min_{x \in \text{case}} \text{first_abnormal}(x) \]

Replay (daily bars): if \(|M_g| \ge 5\), the move was already in the first print, so first_abnormal is the start of the venue's session (Euronext 08:00 UTC, US pre-market 09:00 UTC). Otherwise it is the regular open (US 14:30 UTC). Live (5-minute bars, deferred in the cut): the earliest Lee–Mykland jump,

\[ \mathcal L_t = \frac{r_t}{\hat\sigma_t}, \quad \hat\sigma_t^2 = \frac{1}{K-2}\sum_{j=t-K+2}^{t-1} |r_j|\,|r_{j-1}|, \quad \text{jump} \iff \frac{|\mathcal L_t| - C_n}{S_n} \gt \beta^* = -\log(-\log 0.99) = 4.6001 \]

With \(K = 270\), \(c = \sqrt{2/\pi}\), \(C_n = \frac{(2\log n)^{1/2}}{c} - \frac{\log\pi + \log\log n}{2c(2\log n)^{1/2}}\) and \(S_n = \frac{1}{c(2\log n)^{1/2}}\). The bipower variance in the denominator is robust to the jump itself. When unsure, choose the earlier time.

Proof · the earlier-time rule can never admit hindsight derived

Let \(A(\tau) = \{o : \text{first_seen}(o) \lt \tau\}\). If \(\tau_1 \le \tau_2\), then \(\text{first_seen}(o) \lt \tau_1\) implies \(\text{first_seen}(o) \lt \tau_2\), so \(A(\tau_1) \subseteq A(\tau_2)\). If the true cutoff is \(\tau_{\text{true}}\) and the rule picks \(\hat\tau \le \tau_{\text{true}}\), every admitted record really was seen before the move. The cost of being early is lost evidence, never hindsight. QED For 27 January: \(\tau^* =\) 08:00 UTC, when ASML.AS opened −7.7%.

Events, narratives, phenomena: one rule at three timescales

For a ten-year-old

Now we sort the stapled bundles. Small piles are single happenings: "Nvidia fell 14% before the bell". Boxes hold ongoing stories: "cheap Chinese AI". Big shelves hold whole eras: "the AI boom".

Every box keeps its name forever. If a box gets lumpy, it splits into two boxes and they share its memory fairly. If a box gathers dust for a week, it goes to sleep, and it needs a really good match to wake up again. The sorter only looks at what an article says, never at how popular it is. A discrete nonlinear Hawkes model with divisive competition

For a ten-year-old

Every story is a bell. When news hits it, it rings, and the ringing fades away. There is a fast hum that's gone in an hour and a slow hum that lasts a week. A ringing bell can make the bells next to it ring too: that's one story spilling into another.

But the room only holds so much air. When everything rings at once, every bell gets a bit quieter, by the same fraction, so nobody gets silenced outright.

If a bell suddenly rings far louder than the bells around it could explain, something new came in from outside. We call that a burst, and we say it came through BOT, the door to the outside world.

Proof · Proposition 6.2, divisive competition derived

Write \(c = (B/(B + S^{\text{raw}}))^{\omega}\), so \(\lambda_i = c\,\lambda^{\text{raw}}_i\) and \(S = c\,S^{\text{raw}}\). (a) Ratios: \(\lambda_i/\lambda_k = \lambda^{\text{raw}}_i/\lambda^{\text{raw}}_k\), because c cancels. (b) Capacity: \(c \le 1\) gives \(S \le S^{\text{raw}}\). Writing \(S = (S^{\text{raw}})^{1-\omega} B^{\omega} (S^{\text{raw}}/(B+S^{\text{raw}}))^{\omega}\) gives \(S \le B^{\omega} (S^{\text{raw}})^{1-\omega}\), and at \(\omega = 1\), \(S = B S^{\text{raw}}/(B + S^{\text{raw}}) \lt B\). (c) Monotone: \(dS/dS^{\text{raw}} = c\,(1 - \omega S^{\text{raw}}/(B + S^{\text{raw}})) \gt 0\). (d) Split-consistent: if a parent's raw rate is replaced by children with \(\lambda^{\text{raw}}_a + \lambda^{\text{raw}}_b = \lambda^{\text{raw}}_p\), then \(S^{\text{raw}}\) and c are unchanged, so \(\lambda_a + \lambda_b = \lambda_p\) and every other narrative is untouched. (e) With softplus and non-negative drives, splitting the drive changes the raw total by at most \(2 s \ln 2\). QED

The subtractive alternative fails (d). With \(\lambda_i = (D_i - \kappa S)_+\), splitting p into active children gives \(\lambda_a + \lambda_b = \lambda_p - \kappa S\). Predicted total attention then depends on how finely you clustered, which rules subtraction out of a multi-resolution system (Prop 6.3).

Proof · Lemma 6.5

Shared step. Softplus satisfies \(f(x) = (x)_+ + e(x)\) with \(0 \lt e(x) \le s\ln 2\) (Lemma 6.1). Inhibitory terms only lower η, and \(z \ge 0\), so \((\eta_i)_+ \le (\beta^{\max}_i)_+ + G_{\max}\sum_{j,m} A^+_{ijm} z_{j,m}\). Unrolling the trace gives \(z_{j,m}(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{t} (1-a_m)a_m^{k-1} y_j(t-k) + a_m^t z_{0,j}\), a convex combination of past counts and the initial value. Hence \(\mathbb E[z_{j,m}(t)] \le U_j(t-1) := \max(z_{0,j}, \max_{r \lt t}\mathbb E[y_j(r)])\).

(a) Let \(c_i = s\ln2 + (\beta^{\max}_i)_+\) and \(K = G_{\max}P^+ \ge 0\) with \(\rho(K) \lt 1\). The tower property gives \(\mathbb E[y_i(t)] \le c_i + \sum_j K_{ij} U_j(t-1)\), so componentwise \(U(t) \le \max(U(t-1),\ c + K U(t-1))\). Choose \(\kappa \ge \max U(0)\) and \(w = (I - K)^{-1}(c + \kappa\mathbf 1)\). The Neumann series converges and \((I-K)^{-1} \ge I\), so \(w \ge U(0)\) and \(c + Kw = w - \kappa\mathbf 1 \le w\). By induction, \(U(t-1) \le w\) implies \(c + KU(t-1) \le c + Kw \le w\), so \(U(t) \le w\) for all t.

(b) By Prop 6.2(b), \(S_t \le B_{\max}^{\omega}(S^{\text{raw}}_t)^{1-\omega}\), and the shared step bounds \(S^{\text{raw}}\) by an affine function of the summed traces, \(C_0 + c_1 \sum_{j,m} z_{j,m}\). Since \(x \mapsto x^{1-\omega}\) is concave, Jensen gives \(\mathbb E[\sum_i y_i(t)] \le h(W(t-1))\), where \(h(u) = B_{\max}^{\omega}(C_0 + c_1 M u)^{1-\omega}\) and W is the running maximum of past means. \(h\) is nondecreasing and \(h(u)/u \to 0\), so some \(\bar W\) satisfies \(h(\bar W) \le \bar W\), and induction keeps \(W(t) \le \bar W\). At \(\omega = 1\), \(S_t \lt B_t\) on every path. QED

What it buys: negative couplings, which carry real suppression between stories, never need a stability penalty. And any \(\omega \gt 0\) makes feedback sublinear, which is the formal content of "semi-conservation prevents explosion". It bounds means only. A unique stationary ergodic regime for \(\omega \gt 0\) is conjecture 6.A, so fitted models are simulated and checked for persistent saturation. While \(\hat\omega \lt 0.05\), the fit enforces \(\max_i \sum_j G_{\max}P^+_{ij} \le 0.95\), which bounds \(\rho\) because \(\rho(K) \le\) the max row sum for \(K \ge 0\). Why 4.2 was replaced by 4.2′ · the NB2 loss is not convex counterexample

The original decision 4.2 fitted the NB2 negative log-likelihood: \(\ell(y; \mu) = -y\log\mu + (y + r)\log(\mu + r) + \text{const}\). Its curvature in μ is \(y/\mu^2 - (y+r)/(\mu+r)^2\). At \(y = 0\) that is \(-r/(\mu + r)^2 \lt 0\): concave. Once \(\eta \gg s\), \(\mu \approx c\,\eta\), so the loss is approximately \(r\log(r + c\eta)\), which is concave in the drive. Zero-count windows are the majority here (a mean of about 5 per narrative-window, mostly zeros), so this isn't an edge case. The proofs below (Lemma 6.7, Lemma 8.1) hold for the quasi-Poisson loss \(\ell = \mu - y\log\mu\) and do not transfer to NB2.

Locked fix (4.2′): point estimates are fitted with quasi-Poisson and a Pearson dispersion \(\hat\phi = \frac{1}{n - p}\sum (y - \lambda)^2/\lambda\). NB2, with r estimated by moments, is kept only for the PIT residuals, the intervals and the held-out NB2 log score. That is the research report's original split. \(\hat\phi\) scales the objective but does not move its minimizer, so the fit is unique (Lemma 8.1) and independent of the solver path. Quasi-Poisson needs only the mean and a variance function, so fractional soft-membership counts are admissible, and Poisson QMLE is consistent for count autoregressions even when the true law isn't Poisson (Ahmad & Francq 2016).

Proof · Lemma 6.7, convexity of the quasi-Poisson loss in the drive derived

For \(y \ge 0\) and \(c \gt 0\), \(l(x) = c f(x) - y\log(c f(x))\) with \(f = s\,\text{softplus}(\cdot/s)\) is strictly convex. \(cf\) is strictly convex, since \(f'' = \sigma(1-\sigma)/s \gt 0\). \(-y\log f\) is convex because f is log-concave: \((\log f)'' \le 0\) reduces to \(\log(1 + e^u) \le e^u\), which is \(\log(1+v) \le v\). Composition with the affine map \(\theta \mapsto \eta\) preserves convexity. Hence each row's objective is convex in its parameters, with the competition factor and gain held fixed. QED Lemma 8.1 then gives a unique minimizer: a convex loss, plus norms, plus a strictly convex ridge, over a convex feasible set (sums and maxima of positive parts are convex). Lemma 8.2: the outer loop (refreeze c, re-solve, backtrack, grid-search ω) never increases the true objective, so its values converge. That is convergence of values, not global optimality, because the joint problem is nonconvex in ω.

§6 · Engine 3a · Search · Session 5, locked (5.1–5.13)

The cheapest tree of stories that reaches every abnormal cluster

For a ten-year-old

Think of a treasure map. Every road between two stories has a toll: common roads are cheap, strange ones are expensive. The crashed stocks are treasure chests. We want the cheapest set of roads, starting from the "outside world" gate, that reaches every chest. Sharing a road is cheaper, like friends carpooling.

There is always a direct road from the gate to each chest labelled "I don't know", with a fixed toll. If no story road is cheaper, the honest answer wins.

And so the loudest story can't win just by being loud, we play the same game on 20 boring days when nothing happened, and count how often it "finds" a story anyway.

Solve exactly design · 5.5

push (0, x, {x}) for every terminal x
while heap:
    val, v, X = pop_min(heap)
    if (v, X) done: continue;  mark done; Tval[v, X] = val
    if v == BOT and X == T: return val, backtrack(v, X)            # first pop is optimal (Theorem 12.1)
    for u in parents(v):        push (val + c(u, v), u, X)        # grow: put edge u -> v on top
    for Y finished at v, Y ∩ X = ∅:  push (val + Tval[v, Y], v, X ∪ Y)   # merge two subtrees at v

Proof sketch · Theorem 12.1, the first pop of (BOT, T) is optimal undirected: Dreyfus–Wagner, DPBF directed version

Achievable: every pushed value is the cost of a structure built by grow and merge steps. Where a merge shares nodes, deleting duplicate entering edges gives an arborescence that costs no more. So val ≥ Topt(v, X). Optimal: suppose (v, X) is the first state popped above its optimum. Decompose an optimal arborescence for (v, X) into grow and merge steps down to the base states, which are exact at 0. Some state of that decomposition is unfinished while all its children are finished. When its last child finished, it was pushed with a value at most its optimal sub-cost, which is at most Topt(v, X) < val because costs are ≥ 0. A min-heap would have popped it first, a contradiction. QED Lemma 12.2: every non-root node has exactly one entering edge, so adding \(\lambda_{\text{node}}\) to each edge charges each node exactly once. Quantization: rounding moves each edge by at most 0.0005 nats, so a tree with E ≤ 10 edges moves by at most 0.005 nats, an odds error under 0.5%. Ties are broken by a fixed key, so the result is replay-identical. Feasibility always holds, because the all-abstention tree is feasible.

Why exact, not approximate. Group and directed Steiner problems approximate badly (no \(\log^{2-\epsilon} k\) ratio for group Steiner under standard assumptions; Halperin & Krauthgamer 2003). Exact DP is exponential only in k. With one terminal per cluster, k = 2 on 27 January. The research design's push certificate (Theorem 12.6) and Levin tree search are kept as the path to scale, beyond about 2,000 nodes or once event-level nodes go in. At ~300 narratives, solving the whole graph exactly is cheaper than certifying a local piece.

Competing explanations: a subset, defined precisely design · 5.6, 6.11

\[ \mathrm{OPT}_r = c(\text{BOT} \to r) + \min_{\emptyset \ne X \subseteq T} \Big[ T_{\text{full}}(r, X) + T_{-r}(\text{BOT}, T \setminus X) \Big], \qquad \mathcal R = \{ S_r : \mathrm{OPT}_r \le \mathrm{OPT} + \ln 20 \} \cup \{ S_0 \} \]

For each entry narrative r, a child of BOT, \(S_r\) is the cheapest explanation whose root story enters through r. It is computed exactly from the DP tables (Lemma 11.2): the subtree below r covers some terminals X, and the rest of the tree avoids r. Requiring a different entry makes rivals different stories, not the same chain with one middle node swapped. \(S_0\) is abstention, always shown. Odds between two explanations are \(e^{\Delta\text{cost}}\).

Guarantee (from the definition): if any explanation through root r has odds of at least 1 : 20 against the best, r's best version is in the report, because \(\mathrm{OPT}_r \le\) its cost \(\le \mathrm{OPT} + \ln 20\). Not covered: variations inside one root story (top-2 per root is an option, off by default), and anything outside the graph: non-English press, private information, edges below \(p_{\text{floor}}\). Those don't vanish. They fold into "we do not know".

What the odds mean conjecture 11.A → Lemma 11.1

If each edge were active independently with probability p(e), and each node carried a prior factor \(e^{-\lambda_{\text{node}}}\), then \(P(S) \propto \prod p(e)\, e^{-\lambda_{\text{node}}|S|} = e^{-\text{cost}(S)}\). \(S^*\) would be the MAP explanation, and the odds would be posterior odds. Edges aren't independent, so the odds are a ranking calibrated by placebos, not literal probabilities. In physics terms, cost is energy, \(S^*\) is the ground state, rivals are low-lying excitations, and the odds are Boltzmann factors at unit temperature.

Hub bias: a feature as a prior, a bug as a decision rule design · 5.11–5.13

\[ \text{FER} = \frac{\#\{\text{placebo terminals given a non-abstention explanation}\}}{\#\{\text{placebo terminals}\}} \le 5\% \]
\[ h_u = \frac{\#\{\text{placebo explanations containing } u\}}{\#\{\text{placebo explanations}\}}, \qquad p_{\text{emp}} = \frac{1 + \#\{k : \text{cost}_k \le \text{cost}_{\text{real}}\}}{1 + K} \]

Placebos are K cutoffs at random times in the prior 90 days (case days excluded), with terminals built from instruments that did not move abnormally. \((p_0, \lambda_{\text{node}}, \gamma)\) are tuned so that FER ≤ 5%: fix the false-alarm rate, then maximize detection, which is Neyman–Pearson. Every dossier reports the hub frequency of each narrative it uses (e.g. "appears in 70% of explanations on ordinary days: low diagnosticity") and \(p_{\text{emp}}\): "this explanation fits better than 20 of 20 ordinary days".

Proof · the empirical p-value is valid rank argument; Phipson & Smyth 2010 under exchangeability

Under the null that the real case is just another day, the K + 1 costs are exchangeable, so the real cost's rank among them is uniform on {1, …, K + 1}, with ties counted against the real case. Hence \(P(p_{\text{emp}} \le j/(K+1)) \le j/(K+1)\). The +1 in numerator and denominator is what makes the test exact. Dropping it gives p = 0, which is anti-conservative. QED Resolution: with the hackathon's K = 20, the smallest possible p is 1/21 ≈ 0.048. With 40 placebo terminals, the standard error of a 5% FER is \(\sqrt{0.05 \cdot 0.95/40} \approx\) 3.4 points. The 60 placebos planned after the hackathon bring those to 1/61 and 2.8 points. Exchangeability between a moving and a non-moving instrument is an assumption, not a fact.

Feedback from verdicts (5.8). If Engine 3b rules a claim CONTRADICTED, the edges that depend on it are deleted and the DP reruns. Only the graph changed, so the answer is still exact. This is consistency-based diagnosis: parsimonious consistent explanations are the minimal hitting sets of the conflict sets (Reiter 1987).

§7 · Engine 3b · Evidence and verdicts · Session 6, locked (6.1–6.13′)

Put every story on trial, and prove the verdict

For a ten-year-old

Now every story goes on trial. For each one we search for one clue that would help it and two clues that would hurt it. Looking for what could prove you wrong is how good detectives avoid fooling themselves.

Every fact has to be copied word for word from a real page. Nobody is allowed to make things up. A logic judge then tries every way of believing the witnesses that doesn't contradict itself. If the story survives in all of them, it's SUPPORTED. If it dies in all of them, CONTRADICTED. If it dies in some, UNRESOLVED. If nothing touches it, CONSISTENT-BUT-UNPROVEN.

For one verdict, a math robot (Lean) checks the judge's logic line by line.

Proof · Theorem 14.1, the four verdicts partition every hypothesis derived

Lemma 14.2: every rule with head expl(H) contains not refuted(H). Every stable model is a model of the Clark completion, so expl(H) ∈ M forces some body true in M, hence refuted(H) ∉ M. No model contains both.

Exhaustive: if \(b_{\text{OUT}}\), the verdict is CONTRADICTED when \(c_{\text{OUT}}\) and UNRESOLVED otherwise. If not \(b_{\text{OUT}}\), it is SUPPORTED when \(c_{\text{IN}}\) and CONSISTENT-BUT-UNPROVEN otherwise. Exclusive: \(c_{\text{IN}}\) means every model has expl, so none has refuted (14.2). That gives ¬\(b_{\text{OUT}}\), which rules out UNRESOLVED and CONTRADICTED. \(c_{\text{OUT}}\) with a non-empty model set gives \(b_{\text{OUT}}\), and no model has expl, so ¬\(c_{\text{IN}}\). UNRESOLVED gives a model with refuted, hence without expl, so ¬\(c_{\text{IN}}\). CONSISTENT-BUT-UNPROVEN requires ¬\(b_{\text{OUT}}\), which implies ¬\(c_{\text{OUT}}\) when models exist. QED

Deterministic: brave and cautious consequences are the union and intersection over the set of stable models. That is a unique set, independent of the order in which the solver finds models. Tight: the positive dependency graph (holds → ok, refuted → holds, expl → holds) has no cycle, because ok depends on defeated only through negation. So Fages' theorem applies: stable models are exactly the models of the completion. Complexity: brave reasoning is NP-complete and cautious is coNP-complete for normal programs. The verdict is a fixed Boolean combination of at most five SAT calls, which puts it in \(\Theta_2^P\). Disjunctive rules would raise it to \(\Sigma_2^P/\Pi_2^P\), so the rule language forbids them.

When there is no consistent reading at all

Odd attack cycles can leave no stable model. This is geometric frustration: an antiferromagnetic triangle has no ground state that satisfies every bond. Yggdrasil then emits INCOHERENT-EVIDENCE, with an UNSAT proof that no model exists, and falls back to the grounded labelling, the least fixpoint of the defense operator. It is unique, polynomial, and equal to the well-founded model. A true core atom gives SUPPORTED or CONTRADICTED. An undefined atom with arguments on both sides gives UNRESOLVED. Anything else is CONSISTENT-BUT-UNPROVEN. How often real evidence is incoherent is unknown, and the dossier counts it.

Game theory · the verdict as a debate

Grounded acceptance has an exact game reading (Modgil & Caminada 2009). A proponent claims an argument. An opponent attacks it, the proponent must counter-attack every attack, and the proponent may not repeat an argument. The argument is in the grounded extension iff the proponent has a winning strategy. Equal-tier mutual attacks are the two-player standoff where neither side can force a win, which is why they land in UNRESOLVED. The stable models are the two self-consistent "camps", much like the two pure equilibria of a coordination game (that last part is an analogy, not a theorem). Diagnosticity, the evidence items whose removal flips a verdict (n + 1 solver runs), is the debate's list of decisive moves, and it is Heuer's sensitivity analysis made mechanical.
